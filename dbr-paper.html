Beyond Accuracy: Measuring Reasoning Inconsistency of Large Language Models in Medical Domain

Wonbin Seo

Computational Linguistics & Knowledge Lab, Pusan National University

Paper (soon)
Abstract

As LLMs expand into clinical decision support, reliable reasoning is as critical as accurate answers, yet current accuracy-based evaluation cannot capture reasoning reliability. This study introduces the Trace Inconsistency Rate (TIR), a metric quantifying discrepancies between a model's reasoning trace and its final answer, to evaluate medical LLMs. Using GPT-4o-mini and Qwen-7B, we constructed a multilingual benchmark from MMLU-ProX medical items (1,404 each) and USMLE depression questions (270 each), and built an LLM-as-a-Judge evaluation pipeline. Results show a strong negative correlation between accuracy and TIR (Pearson r = -0.92), with reasoning inconsistency more pronounced in Korean conditions. Human evaluation confirmed the reliability of the automated pipeline (Cohen's κ = 0.756), and this work advances LLM evaluation in the medical domain by introducing reasoning consistency as a complementary assessment criterion beyond accuracy.

Research Log

Why did I think this problem was still open?

My interest was always in how LLMs reason — outputs look plausible, but what happens inside stays opaque. Beg to Differ showed that reasoning does not converge on the same conclusion across languages, and proposed TIR to measure that gap. I had also been asking how far an LLM can replace or support a medical expert. This led to my research question: does the same inconsistency appear where a wrong answer carries clinical cost? No prior work had examined reasoning consistency in the medical domain, so I started there.

What did I define, and what did I give up?

Accuracy tells you an answer was wrong. It does not tell you whether the reasoning behind it held. I classified every output along correctness and consistency into four cases — CC, CI, IC, II — and Case II stood out: errors where the reasoning chain collapsed accounted for 29.43% of all errors, outnumbering cases where coherent reasoning still led to a wrong answer.

The grid locates the problem but does not explain it, so I designed a second layer for why reasoning failed: medical fact error, logical disconnection, information oversight, ambiguous response. This taxonomy is my own contribution.

TIR and accuracy require the full dataset, so I used an LLM-as-a-judge as the backbone and annotated 22 sampled instances myself to validate it. Agreement reached κ = 0.756 on the four-way classification and κ = 0.621 on the binary TIR judgment.

I chose domain over language as my axis. Carrying both variables would make observed inconsistency impossible to attribute to either one.

Where did I have to revise the plan?

My original framing centered on Case CI — inconsistency hidden behind correct answers. A reviewer noted that my results did not support it. I went back to the table and agreed: Case II was what stood out, and outright collapse of reasoning was more common than the pattern I had gone looking for. I rebuilt the argument around it during revision.

Human annotation pointed the same way. One case showed the model recognizing a history of recurrent depression, then selecting the treatment duration for a first episode. Another showed it reading a negatively framed question as positive, skipping the condition immature while still landing on the correct answer. Both supported TIR as a measure that locates reasoning failure independently of accuracy.

What does this result still fail to answer?

Sample size is the weakest point — 22 items is too small to generalize error-type tendencies, so the patterns should be read as preliminary. I was also the only annotator, so no inter-rater reliability could be computed; agreement with the judge is established, but whether my criteria hold for other experts is not. Human validation was limited to English items, leaving the Case IC → Case II pattern in Korean unresolved: it may stem from the model's reasoning or from the annotator's language processing. Extending this work means a larger sample including MMLU-ProX, and cross-validation with multiple expert annotators.

What changed in how I work

I stopped treating a paper as something to close out. Wanting to finish quickly narrowed what I was willing to look at, and that pressure was self-imposed; what I aim for now is the best version reachable in the time available, not the fastest ending. Working through arguments with my advisor also taught me to ask what logical steps are needed to reach the point I want to make, and how to distribute them across paragraphs.
