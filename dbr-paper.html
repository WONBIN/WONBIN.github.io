Beyond Accuracy: Measuring Reasoning Inconsistency of Large Language Models in Medical Domain

Wonbin Seo

Computational Linguistics & Knowledge Lab, Pusan National University

Paper (soon)
Abstract

As LLMs expand into clinical decision support, reliable reasoning is as critical as accurate answers, yet current accuracy-based evaluation cannot capture reasoning reliability. This study introduces the Trace Inconsistency Rate (TIR), a metric quantifying discrepancies between a model's reasoning trace and its final answer, to evaluate medical LLMs. Using GPT-4o-mini and Qwen-7B, we constructed a multilingual benchmark from MMLU-ProX medical items (1,404 each) and USMLE depression questions (270 each), and built an LLM-as-a-Judge evaluation pipeline. Results show a strong negative correlation between accuracy and TIR (Pearson r = -0.92), with reasoning inconsistency more pronounced in Korean conditions. Human evaluation confirmed the reliability of the automated pipeline (Cohen's κ = 0.756), and this work advances LLM evaluation in the medical domain by introducing reasoning consistency as a complementary assessment criterion beyond accuracy.

Research Log

1Why was this problem still open?

The open question was whether reasoning inconsistency also appears in medicine.

LLM reasoning was my starting point. Model outputs can look plausible even when the reasoning behind them is unclear. Beg to Differ showed that LLMs can reach different conclusions across languages and introduced TIR to measure this inconsistency. I wanted to know how far LLMs could support or replace medical experts. This led to my research question: does the same inconsistency appear where a wrong answer carries clinical cost? Previous studies had not tested reasoning consistency in the medical domain.

2What did I define, and what did I exclude?

I measured correctness and consistency separately, then classified why reasoning failed.

Reasoning consistency was the main problem I wanted to measure. Accuracy shows whether an answer is correct, but not whether the reasoning is consistent. I classified each output as CC, CI, IC, or II based on correctness and consistency. The key result was that errors where the reasoning chain collapsed accounted for 29.43% of all errors, outnumbering cases where coherent reasoning still led to a wrong answer.

The four cases showed where a failure occurred, but not why. I therefore created four failure categories: medical fact error, logical disconnection, information oversight, and ambiguous response. This taxonomy is my own contribution.

Measuring TIR and accuracy across the full dataset made human annotation impractical. I used an LLM judge to classify the dataset and manually annotated 22 samples to validate it. Agreement reached κ = 0.756 for the four case classification and κ = 0.621 for the binary TIR judgment.

I chose domain as the main variable. I excluded language because using both variables would make the source of inconsistency difficult to identify.

3How did I revise the plan?

The original focus was Case CI, but the results pointed to Case II.

My original argument focused on Case CI, where inconsistent reasoning still produces a correct answer. A reviewer pointed out that the results did not support this focus. I reexamined the results and agreed. Case II was what stood out, and outright collapse of reasoning was more common than the pattern I had gone looking for. I revised the paper around this finding.

Human annotation supported the revised argument. In one case, the model recognized a history of recurrent depression but selected the treatment duration for a first episode. In another, it misread a negatively framed question as positive and overlooked the condition immature, yet still reached the correct answer. These cases showed that TIR can identify reasoning failures that accuracy alone cannot detect.

4What are the main limitations?

The sample was small, used one annotator, and validated only English items.

Sample size is the weakest point — 22 items is too small to generalize error-type tendencies, so the results are preliminary. I was the only human annotator. I could measure agreement between my labels and the LLM judge, but I could not test whether other experts would apply the criteria in the same way. Human validation included only English items. The shift from Case IC → Case II in Korean therefore remains unresolved. It may reflect the model's reasoning, or it may come from how I interpreted the Korean responses. Future work should use a larger sample, include MMLU-ProX, and compare labels from multiple medical experts.

5How did this project change how I work?

I now prioritize the strongest result I can reach within the available time.

Research quality became more important than finishing quickly. My own pressure to complete the paper limited what I was willing to examine. As a result, what I aim for now is the best version reachable in the time available, not the fastest ending. Discussions with my advisor also changed how I build arguments. I now identify the logical steps needed to support a claim and give each step a clear role in the paper.
